About the treatment

Full-body care. A slower pace.

Swedish massage is a classic full-body oil massage. Your therapist combines long gliding strokes with kneading, friction, rhythmic tapping, vibration and gentle stretching, at a light-to-moderate pressure set for your comfort.

How it works

A rhythm that lets you unwind.

Swedish massage works through a sequence of hand techniques, each with its own job. Long strokes warm the muscles, kneading works into tight spots, and lighter rhythmic movements leave the body loose and settled. Oil lets your therapist's hands glide without drag, so the pressure stays smooth and continuous from start to finish.

Light-to-moderate pressure, all the way through

The pressure stays light to moderate and is adjusted for your comfort. That makes Swedish massage about releasing everyday tension and letting your body relax, rather than working forcefully into the deepest muscle layers. Music, scent and essential oils add to the sense of calm.

The techniques01

Effleurage

smooth, gliding strokes, from light to deeper. It opens the session to warm up the body and returns at the end to soothe the muscles.

02

Petrissage

rolling, squeezing and kneading with the hands, knuckles and fingertips, in circular and upward motions. This takes up the longest part of the session — it works into knots and binding spots and encourages circulation.

03

Friction

deep circular or linear rubbing with the thumbs or fingers, used mainly around the joints to stimulate blood flow to the area.

04

Tapotement

rhythmic tapping with cupped hands, the edge of the hand or the fingertips, including hacking and cupping. It wakes up the muscles and stimulates blood flow.

05

Vibration

rhythmic shaking with the palms over the back and larger muscles, or the fingertips over smaller areas such as the face, to soothe the nerves and soften knotted areas.

06

Stretching

gentle stretches that ease stiffness and help your joints move more freely.

The relaxation response

Space to feel settled.

Long, rhythmic, gliding strokes trigger the body's relaxation response — the slower, calmer state your nervous system shifts into as stress eases. The relaxation response it triggers can leave you calmer and clearer-headed. Studies have linked massage with higher levels of serotonin and dopamine — brain chemicals associated with mood, pleasure and motivation — and lower levels of the stress hormone cortisol. Massage is also thought to prompt the release of endorphins, which may help bring stress hormones down.

Your questions answered

What techniques are used in Swedish massage?

Swedish massage combines gliding strokes with kneading and deep circular movements to stimulate the muscles and release tension. The main techniques are effleurage (gliding strokes), petrissage (kneading, rolling and squeezing), friction (deep circular rubbing), tapotement (rhythmic tapping), vibration (gentle shaking) and stretching.

Who is Swedish massage for?

It suits anyone who wants to ease everyday aches and muscle tension, and anyone who simply wants to unwind with a pampering session. Its light-to-moderate pressure also makes it a good first massage if you're new to it.

How is Swedish massage different from deep tissue massage?

The two share some techniques — the difference lies in pressure and purpose. Swedish massage uses long, gliding strokes at light-to-moderate pressure to help you relax and ease everyday tension. Deep Tissue Massage uses slower, firmer strokes to work into knots and tension held deep in the muscles.

How firm is the pressure?

Light to moderate, adjusted for your comfort. You can ask your therapist to spend more time on particular areas, or to avoid any you'd rather leave alone.

Do I need to undress?

You undress to your comfort level in a private room. You stay covered and kept warm throughout the massage.

How often should I have a Swedish massage?

For maintenance: once or twice a month.
